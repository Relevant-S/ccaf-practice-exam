## Question

A retail support agent works from a policy document. On pricing, that document covers exactly one thing: the company will match a lower price found on its own website within 14 days of purchase.

A customer writes in, politely, asking for a price match against a competitor's listing. They have not asked for a human.

The policy neither permits competitor matching nor forbids it. It simply does not address it.

What should the agent do?

## Options

- **A)** Escalate to a human, because the policy is silent on this request and the agent has no basis on which to decide it.
  - **Rationale:** Correct. A policy gap is a first-class escalation trigger in the exam guide (T5.2), and it is the one most often missed — people assume escalation is for *complex* or *angry* cases. This case is neither. It is simple, the customer is pleasant, and the agent still cannot answer it, because answering would mean inventing company policy. That is a decision only a human is authorised to make.
- **B)** Decline the request, since the policy authorises own-site matching only, and anything not authorised is not permitted.
  - **Rationale:** Reads silence as denial. The document does not say competitor matching is refused; it says nothing at all. Turning an omission into a "no" is the agent deciding policy just as much as saying "yes" would be — it only feels safer.
- **C)** Apply the own-site rule by analogy — the request has the same shape and falls inside the 14-day window.
  - **Rationale:** Stretches a rule past the boundary it was written for. Own-site matching and competitor matching have different commercial consequences, which is presumably why the policy covers one and not the other. Reasoning by shape rather than by authorisation is how agents quietly create liabilities.
- **D)** Ask for the competitor's URL and current price, then decide based on how big the difference is.
  - **Rationale:** Invents a threshold that exists nowhere in the policy. Gathering the details is reasonable *on the way to* a handoff, but making the decision depend on a self-chosen number is a made-up rule dressed up as diligence.
